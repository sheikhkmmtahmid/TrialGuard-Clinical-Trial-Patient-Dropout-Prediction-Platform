Case Study

How I Built & Tested TrialGuard

A walkthrough of the modeling pipeline, where the real data came from, and what happened when I actually tried to break it instead of just reporting the flattering number.

Overview

What I Built and Why

TrialGuard predicts which clinical trial patients are at risk of dropping out. It's a three-stage pipeline: a Cox Proportional Hazards model for survival timing, an XGBoost classifier on top of it for the actual risk score, and SHAP to explain each prediction in plain terms instead of leaving it as a black box.

I started on synthetic data, since it let me control the signal and confirm the pipeline itself was doing what it was supposed to before worrying about anything messier. Once that worked, I went and pulled real, de-identified patient data from public trial repositories to see whether any of it actually held up outside a clean synthetic sandbox.

Mostly, it didn't — and I think walking through exactly why is a better use of this page than pretending it did. Below is the real process: where the data came from, what broke along the way, and the honest numbers once I stopped testing it the easy way.

Architecture

Three Models, Three Different Jobs

"The AI predicts dropout" isn't one thing. Here's exactly which piece does what, and why I picked each one over the alternative.

Cox Proportional Hazards

Estimates when a patient is likely to drop out, not just whether they will.

Two patients can carry the same overall risk, but Cox is what tells you one of them has weeks of runway left and the other has months — the hazard ratio is what the risk tiers and the "60-day early warning" framing are actually built on.

Why this one: Cox has been the standard tool for time-to-event medical data since 1972, and its hazard ratios are something a trial coordinator can read and trust, not a black-box score. Kaplan-Meier, the simpler and even more standard option, only produces a population-level survival curve — it can't take one patient's missed visits or adverse events into account, which defeats the point of a per-patient score. Random Survival Forests and similar ML-based survival models can capture more complex, non-linear relationships, but they need more data per group than this project has — most real sources here average 280 to 450 patients each — so a simpler, more data-efficient model was the more honest choice than a flexible one I couldn't actually feed enough data. I also looked at AFT (accelerated failure time) models, but those require committing upfront to a specific distribution for survival times (Weibull, log-normal, etc.), an extra assumption Cox's semi-parametric approach avoids.

XGBoost

Turns the Cox hazard ratio plus every engineered visit-history feature into one dropout probability per visit.

This is the number that actually drives the risk tiers on the dashboard — missed visits, adverse-event trend, adherence trend, quality-of-life trend, and the Cox hazard ratio all feed into it, and it learns which combinations mattered in the training data.

Why this one: gradient-boosted trees are the standard strong performer on small-to-medium tabular clinical data, and XGBoost specifically has a native, exact SHAP TreeExplainer, so the "why" behind every score on this platform is a real calculation, not an approximation. LightGBM and CatBoost are close, well-regarded siblings in the same family that would likely do comparably well; I haven't specifically benchmarked either against this project's data. Plain logistic regression would be even more directly interpretable, but can't capture interactions the way a tree ensemble can — for example, a missed visit mattering more when adherence is already low. That's a well-established general reason to prefer tree ensembles on this kind of tabular data, not a comparison I ran myself on this dataset, and I want to be upfront about that distinction.

SHAP (TreeExplainer)

Explains, after the fact, exactly why XGBoost gave the probability it gave for one specific patient.

Instead of just showing "62% risk," a patient's page breaks the number down: how many points came from missed visits, from the adverse-event trend, from the Cox hazard ratio, and so on — so a coordinator sees the reasoning, not just a number to trust blindly.

Why this one: SHAP is grounded in Shapley values from game theory, and for tree models specifically, TreeExplainer computes the exact answer rather than an approximation. The main alternative, LIME, is faster but works by sampling nearby synthetic examples and fitting a local approximation, which means asking it twice about the same patient can give a slightly different answer. For a clinical tool, where a coordinator might reasonably check the same patient's explanation more than once, an exact method that gives the same answer every time was the right tradeoff.

One more source is worth naming, because I looked hard at it and specifically decided not to use it. A real Type 1 Diabetes dataset from a hospital in Granada, Spain (736 real patients, over 22 million real glucose readings) looked like a strong candidate for a ninth therapeutic area. But its own published paper only describes early data-collection stops in aggregate — device allergy, death, hospital transfer, or a patient's own decision to stop are all lumped into one sentence, with no field in any of its four data files saying which reason applied to which patient. Every other real source in this project is filtered by exactly that kind of reason field, specifically to keep death and medical/administrative stops out of a "behavioral dropout" label. I checked all four raw files by hand to make sure I wasn't missing a hidden column — there wasn't one. Rather than guess which patients' early stops were genuine dropout, I left the whole source out.

Process

A Few Things I Ran Into

Combining 28 real datasets, each structured its own way, turns up real problems. Here are five I actually hit, not hypotheticals.

Label Leakage

An early version of one of my training labels was accidentally built partly from the outcome it was supposed to predict — a Python operator-precedence mistake (& binds tighter than |). Caught it before it inflated every accuracy number downstream.

Patient IDs Colliding

One multi-site trial reused patient numbers at every site (every site had its own "patient #1"). Matching on that number alone silently merged records from different real people. I caught it because one merged "patient" ended up with 713 recorded side effects, which isn't physically possible for one person.

A Field-Parsing Bug

Some studies recorded sex as "Male"/"Female" or as numeric codes instead of single letters. My parsing only looked for M/F, so it silently marked 65% of the real dataset's gender field "unknown" until I actually went and checked the raw values.

Code and Model Drift

I'd reduced the feature set from 23 to 20 columns in code at some point, but never retrained the deployed model to match — so the live model and the code disagreed about how many inputs to expect. Found and fixed it while writing this page.

An Undocumented Code, Resolved Without Guessing

One real cardiovascular dataset's own codebook only defined 1 of its 4 outcome codes. Rather than guess what the other 3 meant, I cross-checked them against a second field (cause of death) already in the data — every patient coded one way had a real death cause attached, every patient coded the other two ways didn't. That's how "did this person actually leave the study on their own" got answered honestly instead of assumed.

Validation

Testing It the Honest Way

The easy way to test a model trained on data pooled from many hospitals is a random train/test split — mix everyone together, hold out 25%. That gave me 0.75 AUC on real data, which looks fine on its own. But it's a flattering number, because the model can partly cheat: it can learn "which hospital is this patient from" as a shortcut, since each hospital has its own baseline dropout rate that leaks through the other features.

So I reran it holding out four entire hospitals the model had never touched during training or tuning, and tested only on those. That's the harder, more honest test. It dropped the AUC to 0.55 — barely better than a coin flip.

That's the real result, and I'd rather show it than not. I've since more than doubled the real dataset (from 6,476 to 12,435 patients, adding two entirely new therapeutic areas - Parkinson's and heart failure - to what started as an oncology/diabetes/MS collection) and retrained everything, specifically to see whether more real data would close that gap. It didn't move: still 0.55 on new hospitals. More data made the model's reasoning noticeably steadier and made one more risk factor statistically real (see the tables below), but it did not teach the model to generalize to a hospital it's never seen. With roughly 280-450 patients per source on average, and a lot of the extra columns only actually measured for a minority of patients rather than filled in with a placeholder, there isn't enough consistent signal yet for a model to learn something that transfers cleanly from one hospital to another. This is a known, still-unsolved problem in clinical ML in general — models trained across multiple sites routinely struggle to generalize to a new one — not something specific to how this was built, and not something more data alone fixes.

Current Status

What's Actually Running Right Now

The live predictions in this demo come from the synthetic-trained model — the one with the strong numbers above — since it has clean, reliable signal to actually show the full pipeline working: Cox survival estimates, XGBoost risk scores, SHAP explanations, end to end.

The real-data numbers exist to answer the harder question honestly: would this actually work on a hospital it's never seen. Right now, no — and I'd rather say that plainly than dress it up.

Disclosure

Three Fields the Model Never Actually Sees

Distance to trial site, employment status, and prior dropout history are still collected on every patient — the add-patient form, CSV upload, and PDF reports all still ask for and show them — but as of this version, none of the three ever reaches the scaler or either model. Neither Cox nor XGBoost can see them, so they cannot influence a hazard ratio, a dropout probability, or a SHAP explanation, full stop, not just "deprioritized."

This wasn't a guess. I searched by hand across all four independent real-world sources in this project — Project Data Sphere, MUSIC, UCI Heart Failure, and ImmPort — and none of them record any of these three fields for any real patient. Every earlier version of this model that used them only ever had their importance demonstrated on synthetic data built to make them predictive, never confirmed against a real patient's chart. They're kept in the schema, not deleted, so if a future real source ever does supply genuine values for one of them, it can be added back to training without rebuilding any collection or encoding logic.
